Seven kinds of thing

Not everything breaks down the same way.

A goal is not a problem, and a decision is not a risk. Greggle asks what kind of thing you are working on first, because the kind decides what good questions look like. It also decides the word the app uses from then on: Delete this Problem?, not Delete this thing.

All fourteen ways of thinking are always available. The kind decides which come first.

a Goal

Something you are aiming at, without a fixed end date.

say… Make the first team this season.

The word the app uses is Goal, and there is no end date to plan back from, so the first thing suggested is a pre-mortem on the goal itself and the last is a set of key results that would prove you moved.

suggested first

Pre-mortem, Working Backwards, Force Field Analysis, First Principles, Objectives and Key Results

a Project

Something you are setting out to do that finishes.

say… The science fair project.

A Project has an end, so the questions are about what done looks like and what could stop you getting there. Anything you make before choosing a kind is a Project until you say otherwise, and you can change it in one click.

suggested first

Pre-mortem, Working Backwards, Theory of Constraints, Stakeholder and Influence Map, Strategic Challenge Map

a Challenge

Something difficult to win, where the outcome is not yours to control.

say… The debating final.

The outcome sits with someone else, a judge, a market, a panel, so the questions turn outward: what are the challenges, who decides, what could the world do, and how do you put the case.

suggested first

Strategic Challenge Map, Issue Tree, Stakeholder and Influence Map, Scenario Planning, First Principles, Answer First

a Problem

Something that has gone wrong and needs diagnosing.

say… The band keeps missing practice.

Something has already happened, so the questions are about cause. The first thing asked is to state the problem as an observable fact, not a feeling, and then why, and why again.

suggested first

5 Whys, Cause and Effect, Issue Tree, Theory of Constraints, First Principles

a Decision

A choice between options.

say… Which subjects to take next year.

There are options and the danger is that you already like one of them, so the questions insist on what a good answer must do before the options are allowed on the page.

suggested first

Issue Tree, Options and Criteria, First Principles, Force Field Analysis, Pre-mortem, Answer First

an Opportunity

Something that might be worth pursuing, before you have decided to.

say… A stall at the Saturday market.

You haven’t said yes yet, so the questions are about whether to: what is pushing for it and holding it back, what it would have to do to be worth it, and how it looks in four different futures.

suggested first

Force Field Analysis, Options and Criteria, Scenario Planning, Stakeholder and Influence Map, Pre-mortem

a Risk

Something that could go wrong, and has not yet.

say… What if the venue cancels?

It hasn’t happened, so the questions borrow from the methods for things that have: imagine it did and ask why, look across every category of cause, and find the one restraint you could remove.

suggested first

Pre-mortem, Cause and Effect, Force Field Analysis

Not sure which it is?
Has it already gone wrong?A Problem. If it could go wrong but hasn't, a Risk.
Are there options on the table?A Decision. If the only option is whether to start at all, an Opportunity.
Does it finish?A Project. If you'll still be aiming at it next year, a Goal.
Is the outcome someone else's to give?A Challenge.
Still not sure?Pick the nearest and change it later. It is one click, and nothing on the board is lost.

Seven is a start, not a limit. A habit to build, a skill to learn, a conversation you are dreading and a piece of writing each want their own questions, and the four below have theirs on paper already.

Four more kinds, on paper for now

Not in the app yet. The cards need nothing but a pen.

The essay said seven kinds was a start, not a limit. These are the next four, each with the two methods that suit it best, as one-page cards. None of them were invented here either.

a Habit

Something you want to do, or stop doing, without deciding every time.

say… Practise before school every day.

The app has no Habit yet. On paper, the two methods with the strongest evidence behind them are Gabriele Oettingen’s WOOP, which makes you name the obstacle inside you before you plan, and Peter Gollwitzer’s if-then plans, which tie the action to a moment so you never have to decide.

WOOP Gabriele Oettingen, 2014

Wish, outcome, obstacle, plan. Picture the best result, then the thing inside you that gets in the way, then what you will do when it does.

the card · pdf

If-then plans Peter Gollwitzer, 1999

Tie the action to a moment. "If it is 7am and I have eaten, then I practise for twenty minutes." Deciding once, so you never have to decide again.

the card · pdf

a Skill

Something you want to get properly good at, not just do.

say… Solder a clean joint every time.

Getting better and spending hours are different things. Anders Ericsson’s deliberate practice is the method for working at the edge of what you can do with feedback, and the Feynman technique is the check that you understand a thing rather than recognise it.

Deliberate practice Anders Ericsson, 1993

Work at the edge of what you can do, on one specific thing, with feedback you can’t ignore. Hours are not practice.

the card · pdf

The Feynman technique After Richard Feynman; the four steps from around 2011

Explain it in writing to a twelve-year-old. Where you stall, you don’t understand it yet. Go back for exactly that, and try again.

the card · pdf

a Conversation

Something you have to say to someone, and are dreading.

say… Asking the coach why I was dropped.

Two methods from people who studied how conversations go wrong. Chris Argyris’s ladder of inference shows how fast you climb from what you saw to what you concluded, and the three conversations, from the Harvard Negotiation Project, separate what happened from how it felt and from what it says about you.

Ladder of inference Chris Argyris, 1970s

You saw something, picked out part of it, added meaning, assumed, concluded, and acted, in about a second. Climb back down before you speak.

the card · pdf

The three conversations Stone, Patton and Heen, 1999

Every hard conversation is three at once: what happened, how it feels, and what it says about you. Sort them out on paper before you have them out loud.

the card · pdf

a Piece of writing

Something that has to persuade or explain, and is not yet written.

say… The personal statement.

Answer First covers how to deliver an argument. These two are for the page itself: situation, complication, question, answer is the opening that earns the reader’s attention, and the reverse outline is how you find out what a draft actually says once it exists.

Situation, complication, question, answer The consulting introduction, 1970s

Start where the reader already agrees, say what changed, raise the question that change puts in their mind, and answer it. The opening that earns the rest.

the card · pdf

Reverse outline Writing centres everywhere; no single author

Take the draft you have and write one sentence per paragraph saying what it actually says. The list is the argument you actually made, which is rarely the one you meant to.
